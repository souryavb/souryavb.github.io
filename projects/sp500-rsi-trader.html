S&P 500 RSI Mean Reversion Backtester

I built an engine to test whether buying the S&P 500 at RSI extremes beats just holding it. It won two thirds of its trades and still lost to buy-and-hold by 101 percentage points — which turned out to be the interesting part.

The idea

Mean reversion assumes that after a sharp move in one direction, price tends to snap back toward its average. The Relative Strength Index measures how stretched a move is on a 0–100 scale, comparing the size of recent gains to recent losses. Readings below 30 are conventionally called oversold, above 70 overbought.

The strategy is deliberately simple, because a simple rule is one you can actually diagnose when it fails: go long the index when RSI(14) closes at or below 30, and close the position on whichever of three exits comes first.

  • Stop loss — the position is down 5% from entry.
  • Take profit — the position is up 10% from entry.
  • RSI overbought — RSI closes at or above 70, so the snap-back has happened.

Only one position is open at a time, and each entry commits the full cash balance, so the equity curve is a clean record of the rule rather than of a position-sizing scheme layered on top.

How it works

Daily bars come from Yahoo Finance via yfinance. RSI is computed with an exponentially weighted average of gains and losses rather than a simple rolling mean, which is the smoothing Wilder originally specified and the one most charting platforms use — a detail that shifts the signal dates enough to matter.

rsi_trader.py RSI with Wilder smoothing
def compute_rsi(series: pd.Series, period: int = 14) -> pd.Series:
    """Compute Relative Strength Index."""
    delta = series.diff()
    gain = delta.where(delta > 0, 0.0)
    loss = -delta.where(delta < 0, 0.0)
    avg_gain = gain.ewm(com=period - 1, min_periods=period).mean()
    avg_loss = loss.ewm(com=period - 1, min_periods=period).mean()
    rs = avg_gain / avg_loss
    return 100 - (100 / (1 + rs))

The backtest then walks the price series one day at a time, holding cash and shares explicitly rather than working with returns. Walking forward day by day keeps the accounting obvious and makes it hard to accidentally use information the strategy would not have had yet.

What actually happened

Over six and a half years the strategy turned $100,000 into $121,196. Buy-and-hold turned it into $222,300. The win rate was 66.7%.

Those three numbers together are the whole lesson. A strategy can be right about most of its trades and still be a bad strategy, and the win rate is the statistic most likely to hide that from you.

Why it lost

Six trades. Across the whole backtest the rule found six entries — because RSI(14) on a major index rarely closes below 30. It held a position on 171 trading days in total, which means the strategy sat in cash roughly 90% of the time.

That's the entire gap. The period from 2020 to 2026 was mostly a rising market, and a rule that is out of the market nine days in ten cannot capture a rising market no matter how well it picks its moments. It wasn't beaten because its trades were bad — four of six made money. It was beaten because it wasn't there.

The Sharpe ratio of 0.41 says the same thing more quietly. The one genuine advantage is the drawdown: −13.5% against a considerably deeper decline for holding the index through 2022. If your objective were to limit the worst stretch rather than to maximize return, that would be worth something — but that's a different objective than the one I started with, and deciding that after seeing the results would be cheating.

What I'd fix

  • Charge realistic commission and slippage. Fills are assumed at the close, which flatters any strategy — though with six trades it barely moves this one.
  • Stop leaving capital idle. The honest comparison isn't strategy vs. buy-and-hold, it's strategy-plus-cash-yield vs. buy-and-hold, and ignoring the interest on 85% idle cash understates it.
  • Loosen the entry threshold. RSI below 30 is too rare on an index to generate a usable sample; six trades is not enough evidence to conclude anything with confidence, including this.
  • Test across more than one regime. A window dominated by one long recovery cannot tell you how a mean-reversion rule behaves in a sideways market, which is where it should do best.

The dashboard

Re-running a command and squinting at a terminal gets old when you want to know what a different oversold threshold does. A small FastAPI service wraps the same backtest functions and returns price, RSI, the equity curve, and the trade list as JSON, so parameters can be changed in the browser and the result re-read immediately.

shell
$ python rsi_trader.py --start 2020-01-01 --oversold 30 --overbought 70
$ uvicorn server:app --reload --port 8000
