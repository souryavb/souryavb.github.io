S&P 500 RSI Mean Reversion Backtester

Personal project, 2026 · Python, pandas, NumPy, FastAPI

A backtesting engine that buys the S&P 500 when RSI signals oversold and exits on a stop, a profit target, or an overbought reading — then scores the result honestly against buy-and-hold.

  • —%Strategy return
  • —%Buy & hold return
  • —Sharpe ratio
  • —%Max drawdown

The idea

Mean reversion assumes that after a sharp move in one direction, price tends to snap back toward its average. The Relative Strength Index is a standard way to measure how stretched a move is: it compares the size of recent gains to recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Readings below 30 are conventionally called oversold, above 70 overbought.

The strategy is deliberately simple, because a simple rule is one you can actually diagnose when it fails: go long the index when RSI(14) closes at or below 30, and close the position on whichever of three exits comes first.

  • Stop loss — the position is down 5% from entry.
  • Take profit — the position is up 10% from entry.
  • RSI overbought — RSI closes at or above 70, so the snap-back has happened.

Only one position is open at a time, and each entry commits the full cash balance, so the equity curve is a clean record of the rule rather than of a position-sizing scheme layered on top.

How it works

Daily bars come from Yahoo Finance via yfinance. RSI is computed with an exponentially weighted average of gains and losses rather than a simple rolling mean, which is the smoothing Wilder originally specified and the one most charting platforms use — a detail that changes the signal dates enough to matter.

The backtest then walks the price series one day at a time, holding cash and shares explicitly rather than working with returns. Walking forward day by day keeps the accounting obvious and makes it hard to accidentally use information the strategy would not have had yet.

rsi_trader.py RSI with Wilder smoothing
def compute_rsi(series: pd.Series, period: int = 14) -> pd.Series:
    """Compute Relative Strength Index."""
    delta = series.diff()
    gain = delta.where(delta > 0, 0.0)
    loss = -delta.where(delta < 0, 0.0)
    avg_gain = gain.ewm(com=period - 1, min_periods=period).mean()
    avg_loss = loss.ewm(com=period - 1, min_periods=period).mean()
    rs = avg_gain / avg_loss
    return 100 - (100 / (1 + rs))

Scoring it honestly

A total return figure on its own is close to meaningless — a strategy that sits in cash for most of a bull market can post a positive number and still be a bad idea. So every run reports four things next to each other:

  • Return vs. buy-and-hold, because beating zero is not the bar. Beating the index is.
  • Sharpe ratio, annualized from daily returns, for return per unit of volatility.
  • Maximum drawdown, computed from the running peak of portfolio value — the worst stretch you would have had to sit through.
  • Win rate and trade count, which separate a real edge from a handful of lucky trades.

Every closed trade is also recorded individually with its entry, exit, P&L, and the reason it closed. Grouping by exit reason is the most useful diagnostic in the whole project: a strategy whose gains all come from take_profit and whose losses all come from stop_loss is behaving as designed, and one where end_of_data dominates is really just holding the index with extra steps.

The dashboard

Re-running a command and squinting at a terminal gets old quickly when you want to know what a different oversold threshold does. A small FastAPI service wraps the same backtest functions and returns price, RSI, the equity curve, and the trade list as JSON, so parameters can be changed in the browser and the result re-read immediately.

shell
$ python rsi_trader.py --start 2020-01-01 --oversold 30 --overbought 70
$ uvicorn server:app --reload --port 8000

What I'd do next

  • Charge realistic commission and slippage per trade. Right now fills are assumed at the close, which flatters any strategy that trades often.
  • Walk-forward the thresholds instead of fixing them at 30/70, so the parameters aren't quietly fit to the same window they're scored on.
  • Test across more than one regime. A window starting in 2020 is dominated by one recovery, and that is not enough evidence for anything.

This is a research exercise, not investment advice. Backtested results are hypothetical and do not account for commissions, slippage, or taxes.
